# Experiment 10: Phishing-Awareness Simulation

## Aim

To review how a consent-based phishing-awareness exercise can teach users to recognize suspicious messages while protecting participants and their data.

## Requirements and scope

- A written exercise plan and instructor or organization approval
- A mock message and landing page that do not impersonate a real service
- A controlled test mailbox or a static, offline demonstration
- GoPhish only if its use is explicitly approved and restricted to the test environment

Do not send messages to people who have not agreed to participate. Do not collect passwords, tokens, personal information, or other sensitive submissions. Keep any management interface and test campaign isolated from public networks.

## Theory

Phishing-awareness exercises help participants recognize unexpected requests, misleading links, urgency, and unusual sender details. A safe simulation measures learning without capturing secrets. Useful outcome measures include message delivery, aggregate link interaction, and reports of suspicious mail; they should be collected in a privacy-preserving way and not used to shame individuals.

## Procedure

1. Define the learning objective, audience consent, test boundaries, data-retention period, and responsible contact before preparing the exercise.
2. Prepare a clearly controlled mock message and a landing page that explains the training. Do not copy a real service's login page or request credentials.
3. Configure the exercise so that password and form-data capture are disabled. Use only a controlled test mailbox or an offline walkthrough.
4. Verify the message and page with the exercise owner before any approved delivery. Do not use real account credentials, public tracking links, or unapproved sender infrastructure.
5. Review only aggregate, non-identifying results. Delete temporary test data after the exercise and share lessons in a supportive, non-punitive way.

## Result

Record whether the mock exercise met its learning objective and which safe, aggregate measures were available. Do not report identifiable participant behavior or collected credentials.

## Conclusion

A phishing-awareness simulation should prioritize consent, privacy, and learning. Disabling credential collection, limiting delivery to the approved test scope, and reporting aggregate outcomes help prevent the exercise from becoming a real phishing incident.
